Thickness is a mode of its own — in Stereo that slot is “Section” (meaning steel sections). Here the equivalent question is what the slab is made of and how thick it is, and since automatic thickening is a process with settings and a result, it deserves the room.

3Thickness, drawn as thickness

Today the model is a mid-surface: a sheet with no edge. That is a perfectly sound way to solve a shell — the finite elements really do live on the mid-surface — but it is a bad way to look at one, because the single number that tells you whether the design is reasonable is nowhere in the picture.

Mechanically it is simple: each element carries its own t, each node a surface normal, and the drawing offsets by ±t/2 along that normal. Which gives:

  • Faces doubled, plus an edge band. A 32 × 32 mesh goes from 1 024 polygons to 2 048 plus 128 edge quads. Tk has no z-buffer, so these are depth-sorted like everything else in the app; back faces are culled, so the real cost is nearer ×1.2 than ×2.
  • A “draw at true t / exaggerate ×N” switch. At true scale a thin shell reads as a sheet again, which is correct and unhelpful. The exaggeration factor is printed on the canvas whenever it is not 1, so a screenshot can never lie about it.
  • A cutaway. Half the soffit hidden, so you can see under the shell without orbiting beneath it.

4Sections: a list you can live with

You said the section list and display are uncomfortable and that they do not show the thickness. Here is what I would replace them with.

What changes, concretely

  • The cuts persist. They are part of the model, saved with it, exported with it.
  • Three ways to make one: along X, along Y, or by clicking two points on the shell for an arbitrary line — including corner to corner, which on a square plan is the cut that actually explains a corner-supported shell.
  • Live. Drag the plane in the 3D view and the drawing follows.
  • The thickness is the drawing. Not a number in a corner: the profile is the real profile, top fibre and soffit, at true scale.
  • Where thickening acted is marked, with the multiplier it applied.
  • Export: the cut as a DXF polyline pair, or as a table of station / t / N / m / utilisation. Say which you want and I will do that one first.

One detail worth flagging, visible in the figure: the worst utilisation on that cut is not at the thickest point nor at the thinnest, but just where the thickening runs out. That is the kind of thing a section is for, and the kind of thing the current drawing cannot show.

5Supports on the corners of the elements

A corner of an element is a node of the mesh — the same point the solver assembles stiffness at. So clicking one is not an approximation of a support; it is the support, exactly where you put it.

  • Presets: free, pinned, fully fixed, roller in X or Y, and an elastic (spring) support with a stiffness — the realistic boundary for a shell sitting on a ring beam.
  • Quick actions: the four plan corners, the whole boundary, the lowest ring — the three support sets a shell almost always wants.
  • A support sandbox, as in Stereo: switch one support off and re-solve without editing the model, to see what it was carrying.
  • Before the solve, the same boundary check Stereo runs: a floating node, a missing load path or insufficient restraint is reported as a sentence, not as a singular matrix.

6The surface wizard, brought over

This is the cheapest large win in the whole plan, because the hard part is already written and tested on the Stereo branch. expr_math.py (an AST whitelist, no eval), the domain handling, the plan-shape mask, the summit finder and the crossing check are all surface mathematics — none of them know or care whether what gets laid on the surface is a lattice of rods or a mesh of shell elements.

Alongside it, Build mode keeps the ready-made families for when you do not want to write a formula: dome, barrel vault, hypar, cone, groin vault, elliptic dome, paraboloid dish, folded plate and flat slab, each with its own dimensions and a sensible default support set.

7The colour analysis, and a guide to it

Two separate problems here, and they have different fixes. The first is that the list is hidden: eight quantities behind one closed combobox in the top bar. The second is that even once it is open, nothing anywhere says what any of them means — and for a shell that matters much more than for a truss, because a truss rod has one number (axial force) while a shell element has membrane forces, bending moments, transverse shears, stresses on two different faces, and a utilisation that combines them.

The eight modes, and the sentence each one gets

Bending moment m₁ / m₂moment per metre across the thickness where the shape failed to work as a shell and it is acting as a plate
Transverse shear q₁ / q₂out-of-plane shear per metre near supports and ribs; the check a thin shell rarely fails and a thick one can
Von Mises, top / bottom fibrecombined stress on one face which face governs — the answer is not the same over a support as at midspan

The guide text lives in one place and is shown in three: the one-liner in the panel, the card behind the ?, and a full guide page in REPORTS AND GUIDES/ in the same format as the Stereo surface guide. One source, so they cannot drift apart.

Three smaller things about the legend

  • It prints numbers, at both ends and the middle, in your unit convention.
  • It counts what it flattened. Stereo's legend says “116 rods below 3% of the scale”; here it would say how many elements are carrying almost nothing, which is the difference between a pale model and a failed drawing.
  • The scale anchor is a control, not a secret: this model's peak, its 95th percentile, or a number you pin — and when it is pinned, everything past the end of the ramp is hatched rather than clamped silently.

8Under the hood: what the solver has to be

This section only applies if the old app does not arrive. If it does, its solver is ported as-is and this is moot. I am writing it out anyway so that you can see what I would be choosing on your behalf, and object.

The element

A curved shell meshed into flat facets, each facet a membrane + plate bending element: MITC4 for quads and DKT for triangles. Both are the standard robust choices — MITC4 does not lock in shear as the shell gets thin, DKT is the reference thin-plate triangle — and between them any mesh the wizard can produce is covered. Six degrees of freedom per node (three translations, three rotations, the drilling one stabilised), which is what makes “restrain θx here” a meaningful instruction rather than a no-op.

What it produces, per element

N₁₁, N₂₂, N₁₂ · m₁₁, m₂₂, m₁₂ · q₁, q₃ · principal values and their directions · stresses on the top and bottom fibre · von Mises on each face. Per node: three displacements, three rotations, and reactions at every restrained freedom. Every colour mode in §7 is a projection of that list — none of them needs a separate solve.

Size and speed, honestly

A 32 × 32 quad mesh is 1 089 nodes × 6 = 6 534 unknowns. Dense, that matrix is 340 MB and the solve is minutes; sparse, it is a few MB and well under a second. SciPy is already a hard dependency of this project (requirements.txt says so, for Cable Web), so the assembly is sparse from the start. A 64 × 64 mesh — 25 350 unknowns — still solves in a second or two. Above that the Tk canvas, not the solver, is the limit, so the Mesh panel states the element count before you press Generate and warns past a threshold.

This is question 5

What material and code should the check be against? The rest of the app is steel to CIRSOC 301. A concrete shell needs a completely different check (cracked section, minimum reinforcement, and the thickening rule would be about reinforcement ratios, not about stress). I can do either, but not both at once, and the choice changes the Thickness mode substantially.

9Automatic thickening, made inspectable

Right now, as you put it, you cannot see where the thickening is happening. The fix is not only to draw it — §3 and §4 do that — but to make the process itself something you can read and argue with. What I would build:

  • The rule is stated in the panel, in one sentence, with its criterion named: thicken where utilisation exceeds a target, or where a moment exceeds a value, or to hold a stress on the governing fibre.
  • Its limits are yours: minimum t, maximum t, the step it is allowed to move in (a shell built in 25 mm increments is buildable; one built in 3.7 mm increments is not), and a smoothing radius, because a thickness map that jumps element to element is a drawing nobody can pour.
  • It iterates and reports: pass 1 raised 84 elements, pass 2 raised 11, pass 3 none — converged. A run that does not converge says so instead of stopping quietly at a limit.
  • It leaves a map behind, which is what lets §4's section drawings band the zones it acted on and print the multiplier it applied there.
  • It can be turned off and compared: hold the before and after, and show the pair — weight, peak deflection, governing utilisation, and where the governing element moved to.

If your existing rule is different from any of this, yours wins — send it and I will keep it exactly, and spend the work on making it legible instead. A thickening rule is a design decision, not an implementation detail, and I would rather not invent one for you.

10How I would build it

Six stages. Each one ends with the app running and its tests passing, so you can stop me at any boundary and still have something that works. Tests go in tests/ beside the 54 files already there and run in the same pytest suite.

Stage 1

The skeleton and the solver. apps/shell/, a new tab in main.py, the mesh data model, the element, the assembly, the boundary check. Verified against cases with known answers: a simply supported square plate under uniform load (Timoshenko), a clamped circular plate, a cylindrical barrel vault, and a patch test the element must pass exactly.

No UI yet beyond a Generate and an Analyze. The numbers have to be right first.

Stage 2

The window. Mode rail, one context panel, canvas, status bar, view cube, cards, Alt+1…9, focus rings — the Stereo shell, adapted. Build mode with the ready-made families.

This is the stage where it starts to feel like the Stereo tab.

Stage 3

Thickness in 3D, and supports on the corners. The solid drawing, the exaggeration switch, the cutaway; picking, presets, the six freedoms, the quick actions, the sandbox.

The two things you asked for that change the picture most.

Stage 4

Sections. The mode, the persistent cut list, the live plane, the true-scale drawing, the stations, the thickening bands, export.

Stage 5

Shape mode and the wizard. Ported from the Stereo branch: expression parser, keypad, domains, plan-shape rule, summit finder, crossing check.

Cheap, because it is mostly a move — but it depends on the Stereo branch being merged first, or on me copying the modules across. See question 8.

Stage 6

Colour, the guide, thickening, reports. The docked list, the ? cards, the guide page, the thickening panel and its convergence report, the element table, Excel export, and the worked examples.

11What this plan does not include

Stated plainly so that nothing is assumed:

  • Linear, small-deflection only. No geometric non-linearity. This matters more for shells than for frames: a thin shell's real failure mode is often snap-through buckling, and a linear solve will not see it. I would print a warning when the peak deflection passes a fraction of the thickness, which is the point where the linear answer stops meaning much.
  • No form-finding. The shape is yours to specify; the app will not find the funicular surface for a load case. Say the word and it becomes its own project — it is a good one.
  • No buckling capacity beyond whatever member-style check the code gives.
  • One load case at a time, as in every other tab.
  • No dynamic, thermal or staged-construction analysis.
  • No ribs or edge beams as separate elements in the first pass — a stiffened edge is modelled by thickening it. If you want real beam elements along the shell edges, that is worth doing, and it is question 9.
